# Response prevention strategies to use at home

## What response prevention means

Response prevention is a skill used in therapy to help reduce anxiety, fear, distress, or uncomfortable urges over time. It means choosing not to do the action you usually do to get quick relief. That action might be checking, avoiding, asking for reassurance, washing, repeating, fixing, confessing, or using another habit that helps for a moment but keeps the problem going in the long run.

The goal is not to “just suffer” or force yourself to feel miserable. The goal is to teach your brain that you can handle discomfort safely, and that the feared outcome often does not happen, is less likely than it feels, or can be tolerated without doing the usual ritual or safety behavior. With practice, anxiety and urges often become less intense and pass more quickly.

Response prevention is often used along with exposure, which means gradually facing situations, thoughts, feelings, or sensations that trigger distress. Together, these skills can help break the cycle of fear and avoidance.

## How the cycle works

Many emotional problems are maintained by a repeating loop:

A trigger happens. You feel anxiety, doubt, disgust, guilt, panic, or a strong urge. You do something to feel better fast, such as avoid, check, ask, wash, or mentally review. Relief comes briefly. Then your brain learns, “I needed that behavior to be safe.” The next time, the trigger feels just as strong or stronger.

Response prevention interrupts this loop. When you do not perform the usual response, your brain gets the chance to learn something new: discomfort can rise and fall on its own, and you can cope without the ritual or safety behavior.

## Common examples at home

Response prevention can look different for different people. Examples include:

Not going back to recheck locks, appliances, or messages after you already checked once.

Leaving an item slightly out of place instead of arranging it “just right.”

Touching a household surface and waiting before washing if there is no real contamination risk.

Reading an email once before sending instead of reviewing it over and over.

Allowing uncertainty after making a decision instead of asking others for reassurance.

Staying in a feared situation long enough for anxiety to settle instead of leaving right away.

Not mentally replaying a conversation to figure out whether you said something wrong.

These examples should always be adjusted to fit your treatment plan and your actual level of risk. Response prevention is not about ignoring real danger, hygiene needs, or medical advice.

## Getting ready to practice

It helps to make a plan before you begin. Start by identifying the behaviors you want to reduce. Be specific. Instead of writing “stop checking,” write “check the stove one time, then leave the kitchen.” Instead of “stop reassurance,” write “wait 20 minutes before asking if everything is okay.”

Next, rate situations from easier to harder. This is often called a hierarchy. Start with something challenging but manageable, not the hardest item on your list. Early success builds confidence.

Choose when and where you will practice. Repetition matters. Short, regular practice usually works better than waiting until you feel “ready.”

It can also help to write down what you predict will happen if you resist the behavior. After practice, compare what you predicted with what actually happened. This helps your brain learn from experience rather than from fear.

## What to do during practice

When a trigger happens, notice the urge to do your usual behavior. Pause. Name what is happening: “I’m feeling anxious,” “I’m having the urge to check,” or “My mind is asking for certainty.” Try to observe the experience rather than argue with it.

Then follow your plan to delay, reduce, or stop the response. You might:
wait 5 to 10 minutes before acting,
limit yourself to one check,
stay in the situation without escaping,
or allow the thought to be there without trying to cancel it.

Expect discomfort. Anxiety, tension, guilt, or doubt may rise at first. This does not mean the practice is failing. In many cases, it means you are doing the work. Try to let the feelings rise and fall like a wave. Focus on allowing rather than fixing.

Use simple coping skills if needed, but not in a way that becomes a hidden ritual. Helpful options include slow breathing, grounding through the senses, relaxed posture, or gently returning attention to the present moment. The purpose is to stay engaged, not to make all discomfort disappear right away.

## Important guidelines

Practice long enough for learning to happen. In many cases, this means staying with the situation until the urge becomes more manageable or until you have chosen not to do the ritual for the planned amount of time. You do not have to feel calm before ending practice, but it helps not to stop at the peak of panic if you can safely remain longer.

Repeat the same exercise many times. New learning grows through repetition.

Reduce safety behaviors too. Safety behaviors are small things you do to feel protected, such as carrying “just in case” items, distracting yourself constantly, asking indirect reassurance questions, or making rules that let the ritual continue in a less obvious form. These behaviors can keep fear going even when they seem minor.

Aim for progress, not perfect performance. If you do part of a ritual or give in after delaying, that is still useful information. Notice what happened, learn from it, and try again.

## Handling thoughts and urges

Trying to force scary thoughts away often makes them stronger. Instead, practice letting thoughts be present without treating them like emergencies. A thought is not the same as an action, intention, or prediction.

You might say to yourself, “Maybe, maybe not,” “I can handle uncertainty,” or “This is an anxious thought, not a command.” The goal is not to convince yourself that nothing bad could ever happen. The goal is to stop feeding the cycle by demanding total certainty.

Urges also pass. An urge can feel powerful without needing to be obeyed. Notice where you feel it in your body. Breathe. Let time pass. Many people find that urges peak and then soften if they are not acted on.

## Tracking your progress

Keep a simple log of your practice. Write down the trigger, what response you resisted, how strong your distress or urge felt at the start, and what happened over time. Also note any learning, such as “Anxiety dropped from 8 to 4 in 15 minutes,” or “I asked for less reassurance than usual and nothing bad happened.”

Tracking helps you see gains that are easy to miss day to day. It also helps your therapist adjust the plan if needed.

## Family and support at home

If you live with others, let them know how they can help. Support usually works best when others are calm, encouraging, and consistent. In many cases, loved ones should try not to provide repeated reassurance, participate in rituals, or help you avoid triggers. That can be hard at first, but it often supports recovery.

Instead, they can praise effort, remind you of your plan, and encourage you to use your skills.

## When to get extra help

Talk with your therapist if practices feel too hard, if you are getting stuck, or if you are unsure what counts as a ritual or safety behavior. Seek urgent help right away if you have thoughts of harming yourself or others, feel unable to stay safe, or your symptoms suddenly become much worse.

Response prevention works best when it is gradual, consistent, and guided by a clear plan. With practice, many people find that urges become easier to tolerate, anxiety becomes less controlling, and daily life opens up again.
