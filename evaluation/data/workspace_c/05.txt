# Sleep problems and anxiety

## Why sleep and anxiety affect each other

Sleep and anxiety often go hand in hand. When you feel anxious, your mind and body may stay on high alert. You may notice racing thoughts, muscle tension, a fast heartbeat, or trouble relaxing. These symptoms can make it hard to fall asleep or stay asleep.

Poor sleep can also make anxiety worse. After a bad night of sleep, it is common to feel more irritable, emotionally sensitive, and less able to cope with stress. This can create a cycle: anxiety disrupts sleep, and lack of sleep increases anxiety.

The good news is that both problems usually improve when they are treated together. Small changes in habits, thoughts, and routines can make a real difference over time.

## Common sleep problems

People with anxiety may have different kinds of sleep difficulties. Common problems include:

Trouble falling asleep because the mind feels busy or worried.

Waking up during the night and struggling to fall back asleep.

Waking up too early and feeling unable to return to sleep.

Sleep that feels light, restless, or not refreshing.

Daytime tiredness, poor concentration, low patience, or feeling “on edge.”

Sometimes people also develop habits that seem helpful in the moment but make sleep worse later. These may include going to bed very early, sleeping late, taking long naps, checking the clock often, or using alcohol to try to relax.

## Signs anxiety may be affecting your sleep

Anxiety-related sleep problems often include worry at bedtime, fear about not sleeping, or a strong sense of dread when night approaches. Some people begin to think, “If I do not sleep well, tomorrow will be a disaster.” These thoughts are understandable, but they can increase pressure and make sleep harder.

You may also notice physical symptoms at night, such as a tight chest, upset stomach, sweating, shallow breathing, or restlessness. These can be signs that your body is staying in a stress response when it needs to wind down.

## Healthy sleep basics

Good sleep habits are an important first step. These habits are sometimes called sleep hygiene. They do not solve every sleep problem on their own, but they can support better sleep.

Try to wake up at about the same time every day, including weekends. A steady wake time helps set your internal clock.

Go to bed only when you feel sleepy, not just because the clock says it is bedtime.

Use the bed mainly for sleep and sex. Avoid working, scrolling, watching stressful shows, or worrying in bed if possible.

Keep naps short, or avoid them if they make it harder to sleep at night. If you nap, try to keep it earlier in the day.

Limit caffeine, especially in the afternoon and evening. Caffeine can stay in the body for many hours.

Avoid nicotine close to bedtime. It can be stimulating.

Avoid alcohol as a sleep aid. It may make you drowsy at first, but it often leads to lighter sleep and more waking during the night.

Get regular physical activity during the day. Exercise can improve sleep and anxiety, but try not to do intense exercise right before bed if it keeps you energized.

Keep your sleep space as dark, quiet, and comfortable as you can.

## Calming the mind before bed

Many people with anxiety benefit from a wind-down routine. Your brain needs time to shift from “doing” to “resting.” Try setting aside 30 to 60 minutes before bed for quiet, predictable activities.

Helpful choices may include dimming lights, taking a warm shower, gentle stretching, reading something calming, or listening to soft music.

If racing thoughts are a problem, set a “worry time” earlier in the evening. Spend 10 to 15 minutes writing down worries and possible next steps. When worries show up later, remind yourself that you already gave them time and can return to them tomorrow.

Relaxation skills may also help. Slow breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, mindfulness, and grounding exercises can reduce physical tension. These skills work best with practice, not perfection. The goal is not to force sleep but to help your body become more settled.

## What to do if you cannot sleep

Lying in bed awake for a long time often increases frustration and worry. If you have been awake for what feels like about 20 minutes or more, get out of bed and do something quiet and low light. You might sit in another room and read something simple or listen to something calm. Return to bed when you feel sleepy again.

Try not to watch the clock. Clock-checking can increase anxiety and make the night feel longer.

It can also help to notice unhelpful sleep thoughts. Examples include “I must get eight hours or I will not function,” or “I will never fall asleep.” These thoughts can feel true in the moment, but they are often extreme. A more balanced thought might be, “I prefer more sleep, but I have gotten through hard days before,” or “Resting quietly is still helpful.”

## Anxiety treatment can improve sleep

If anxiety is a major part of the problem, treating the anxiety directly is important. Outpatient therapy can help you understand the cycle between thoughts, feelings, body sensations, and behaviors.

Therapy may include learning coping skills, practicing relaxation, identifying patterns of worry, and changing habits that keep sleep problems going. Cognitive behavioral approaches are commonly used and can be very effective for both anxiety and insomnia.

In some cases, medication may also be part of treatment. Medication decisions should be made with a licensed medical provider who can review benefits, risks, side effects, and how a medication may affect sleep.

## When to get medical advice

Not all sleep problems are caused by anxiety. It is important to talk with a medical provider if you snore loudly, stop breathing during sleep, gasp for air, have frequent nightmares, sleepwalk, act out dreams, or have uncomfortable leg sensations that worsen at night.

You should also seek medical advice if sleep problems last more than a few weeks, cause major daytime impairment, or come with severe depression, panic, substance use, or thoughts of self-harm.

A medical review can help rule out other causes such as sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, pain, medication effects, thyroid problems, or other health conditions.

## Be patient with the process

It is normal for sleep improvement to take time. When people have struggled with sleep for a while, it can take repeated practice to build a healthier pattern. Progress is often gradual rather than immediate.

Try to focus on consistency more than perfect sleep. A calm routine, a steady wake time, and kinder responses to anxious thoughts can help retrain your body and mind.

If sleep and anxiety are affecting your daily life, you do not have to manage it alone. Support, therapy, and practical strategies can help you feel safer, calmer, and more rested.
