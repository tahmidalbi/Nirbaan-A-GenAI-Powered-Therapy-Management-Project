# Health anxiety and repeated body checking

## What this handout is about

Many people notice changes in their body and wonder what they mean. This is normal. Health anxiety happens when normal concern about health becomes frequent, upsetting, and hard to control. A person may spend a lot of time worrying that they have a serious illness, even when medical evaluations have been reassuring or symptoms are minor, common, or unexplained.

One behavior that often keeps health anxiety going is repeated body checking. This handout explains what body checking is, why it can make anxiety worse, and what can help.

## What is body checking?

Body checking means repeatedly examining your body for signs of illness, injury, or something being “wrong.” It can be obvious or subtle. Examples include:

- Feeling your neck, breasts, abdomen, or lymph nodes over and over
- Looking at your skin, eyes, tongue, or stool for changes
- Taking your pulse, blood pressure, temperature, or oxygen level many times
- Testing strength, balance, breathing, swallowing, or memory again and again
- Comparing one side of the body to the other
- Checking online images to see whether something looks normal
- Asking others to inspect a body part or reassure you

Some checking is reasonable. For example, following medical advice for monitoring a symptom can be appropriate. The problem is not awareness of your body. The problem is repeated checking driven by fear, especially when it is done to feel certain or immediately less anxious.

## Why people keep checking

Most people check because they want relief. You may notice a sensation, have a worried thought, and then feel a strong urge to examine your body. When you check, you may feel better for a moment. That short-term relief can make checking more likely next time.

This creates a cycle:

A body sensation or thought appears.  
You feel anxious.  
You check, search, compare, or ask for reassurance.  
Anxiety drops briefly.  
Soon the doubt returns, often stronger than before.

Over time, the brain learns that the sensation must have been dangerous because it needed so much attention. This can make you more alert to normal body sensations, such as muscle tension, stomach activity, heartbeat changes, fatigue, or brief pain. The more closely you watch your body, the more you notice, and the more alarming things may seem.

## How repeated checking can make anxiety worse

Repeated body checking can increase distress in several ways.

First, it keeps your attention locked on the body. Everyone has normal changes and sensations throughout the day. When you scan for problems, you are more likely to notice them.

Second, checking can make sensations stronger. Pressing on an area can make it sore. Repeatedly testing breathing can make you feel short of breath. Monitoring pulse can make you notice every skip or increase.

Third, checking does not create lasting certainty. Most health worries cannot be solved by examining the body at home. Instead, checking often leads to more doubt: “What if I missed something?” or “What if it changed since last time?”

Fourth, repeated checking can interfere with daily life. It may take time away from work, family, sleep, exercise, and enjoyable activities. It can also lead to frequent internet searching, repeated messages to medical providers, or repeated requests for reassurance from loved ones.

## Common thinking patterns

Health anxiety often involves certain habits of thinking. These are not a sign of weakness. They are common patterns the brain can fall into when it feels threatened.

You may assume the worst-case explanation for a sensation. A headache becomes a brain tumor. A flutter becomes a heart problem. A rash becomes a serious disease.

You may overestimate how likely a serious illness is. You may also underestimate your ability to cope with uncertainty.

You may believe that if you do not keep checking, you will miss something important and it will be too late. This can make checking feel responsible, even when it is actually increasing fear.

## What helps

The goal is not to ignore your health. The goal is to respond in a balanced way that protects both physical health and emotional well-being.

One of the most effective treatments is cognitive behavioral therapy, often called CBT. CBT helps people notice worry patterns, test anxious predictions, and reduce behaviors that keep the anxiety going. For health anxiety, a key part of treatment is often reducing body checking and reassurance seeking.

Exposure-based strategies may also help. This means gradually facing health-related uncertainty without doing the usual checking or reassurance behaviors. With practice, anxiety tends to rise and then fall on its own. Over time, the brain learns that uncertainty can be tolerated and that danger was overestimated.

Some people also benefit from medication, especially if anxiety is intense, persistent, or happening along with depression or panic symptoms. A prescribing clinician can discuss options.

## Steps you can try

Start by observing your pattern. For a few days, notice when you check, what triggered it, and how anxious you felt before and after. This can help you see the cycle clearly.

Next, choose one checking behavior to reduce. It is usually easier to start small. For example, if you check a lump ten times a day, aim to reduce to eight, then six, and so on. If you take your pulse many times, set a limit.

Delay the urge. When you want to check, wait five or ten minutes first. During that time, do something else: walk, stretch, call a friend, fold laundry, or focus on your surroundings. Often the urge will lessen.

Set planned check times only if medically appropriate and if this is part of a broader reduction plan. Random checking throughout the day tends to keep anxiety high.

Reduce reassurance seeking. Try asking loved ones not to repeatedly confirm that you seem okay. Reassurance helps briefly but can strengthen anxiety over time.

Limit online searching. Looking up symptoms often increases fear because serious conditions are easier to find than common explanations.

Practice more balanced self-talk. You might tell yourself: “This is a sensation, not a diagnosis.” “My anxiety is making this feel urgent.” “I can let this be here without checking right now.”

## Coping with uncertainty

A major part of recovery is learning to live with some uncertainty. No one can have total certainty about their health. Trying to get complete certainty usually fuels more anxiety.

You do not need to convince yourself that nothing is wrong. Instead, try aiming for a more realistic position: “There may be many possible explanations, and I do not need to solve this immediately.” This is often more helpful than arguing with every worried thought.

Mindfulness skills can also help. Notice the sensation or thought, name it, and bring your attention back to the present task. The goal is not to make the thought disappear. The goal is to respond differently.

## When to seek medical care

This handout is not a substitute for medical advice. It is important to seek medical evaluation for new, severe, or clearly worsening symptoms, or if you have symptoms your medical clinician has told you need prompt attention. Follow the guidance of your healthcare professionals, including recommended screenings and follow-up.

If you already have a medical condition, health anxiety can still be present. In that case, it can be helpful to work with both medical and mental health clinicians so you can follow appropriate care without getting pulled into excessive checking.

## When to seek mental health support

Consider therapy if health worries are taking up a lot of time, causing distress, affecting relationships, disrupting sleep, or leading to repeated checking, searching, or reassurance seeking. Treatment can help you feel less trapped by fear and more able to respond calmly to normal body sensations.

Change usually happens gradually. The aim is progress, not perfection. Each time you resist checking, delay reassurance, or allow uncertainty to be present, you are teaching your brain a new pattern. Over time, this can reduce anxiety and help you return your attention to the life you want to live.
