# Panic attacks and interoceptive exposure

## What is a panic attack?

A panic attack is a sudden rush of intense fear or discomfort that builds quickly. It often comes with strong physical sensations, such as a pounding heart, shortness of breath, dizziness, chest tightness, sweating, shaking, nausea, tingling, or feeling unreal or disconnected. Many people also have frightening thoughts during a panic attack, such as “I’m going to pass out,” “I’m having a heart attack,” or “I’m losing control.”

Panic attacks are very uncomfortable, but they are not dangerous by themselves. The body is reacting as if there is an emergency, even when there is no actual threat. This is the body’s alarm system turning on too strongly or at the wrong time.

Some people have panic attacks only once in a while. Others begin to fear the attacks so much that they change their lives to avoid them. They may avoid exercise, crowds, driving, being alone, or places where escape feels hard. This can shrink daily life and keep panic going.

## Why panic keeps happening

Panic often becomes a cycle. A person notices a body sensation, such as a skipped heartbeat, lightheadedness, warmth, or faster breathing. The sensation is then interpreted as dangerous: “Something is wrong.” That fear causes more adrenaline and more body sensations. The sensations become stronger, which increases fear, and the cycle snowballs into panic.

Avoidance and “safety behaviors” can accidentally keep this cycle going. Safety behaviors are things people do to try to prevent disaster, such as sitting down right away, carrying water “just in case,” checking pulse repeatedly, leaving situations early, or avoiding stairs or caffeine completely. These behaviors can bring short-term relief, but they teach the brain that the sensations really were dangerous and had to be escaped. Over time, fear grows stronger.

## What is interoceptive exposure?

Interoceptive exposure is a treatment method used in cognitive behavioral therapy for panic. “Interoceptive” means related to sensations inside the body. In this treatment, a person purposely brings on harmless physical sensations that are similar to those felt during panic, in a planned and gradual way.

The goal is not to make you suffer. The goal is to teach your brain a new lesson: these sensations are uncomfortable, but they are safe and temporary. By practicing without escaping or using safety behaviors, you learn that the sensations rise and fall on their own and do not lead to catastrophe.

For example, if dizziness is scary, a therapist may guide you to spin in a chair briefly. If a racing heart is scary, you may do short bursts of exercise. If shortness of breath is scary, you may practice breathing through a narrow straw for a brief period. Each exercise is chosen carefully and used in a structured way.

## Why this treatment works

Panic disorder is often fueled by fear of the sensations themselves. Interoceptive exposure helps reduce that fear. Repeated practice can make the sensations feel more familiar and less alarming. You learn from experience, not just logic, that your body can handle these changes.

This treatment also helps break the habit of avoidance. Instead of fleeing from sensations, you approach them on purpose. That approach sends a powerful message to the brain: “This is not an emergency.”

Many people notice that panic becomes less frequent, less intense, and less controlling when they stop fighting the sensations and start allowing them.

## What to expect in therapy

Treatment usually begins with education about panic and the body’s alarm system. You and your therapist may identify the sensations you fear most, the thoughts that show up during panic, and the situations you avoid.

Next, you may create a list of feared sensations and rate how scary each one feels. Then you begin exposure exercises, often starting with sensations that feel challenging but manageable. During each exercise, you practice staying with the sensation long enough to notice that it peaks and passes. Afterward, you review what happened, what you predicted, and what actually occurred.

Your therapist may also help you notice and reduce safety behaviors. For example, if you always sit down when dizzy, one practice goal may be to stay standing safely for a short time. If you constantly check your heart rate, you may practice delaying or stopping that checking.

Homework between sessions is common. Repetition matters. The more consistently you practice, the more your brain learns.

## Common interoceptive exercises

Exercises vary based on your symptoms and health history. Common examples include brisk stepping or jogging in place to increase heart rate, spinning to create dizziness, head movements to trigger lightheaded feelings, breathing through a straw briefly to mimic shortness of breath, muscle tensing to create shakiness, or holding the breath for a short time. Some people practice running up stairs, sitting in a warm room, or wearing a scarf loosely around the neck if throat sensations are a trigger.

These exercises are not random. They are chosen to match the sensations you fear. They are done for brief, planned periods with attention to learning rather than performance.

## How to respond during exposure

During interoceptive exposure, try to take an observing stance. Notice what your body is doing. Name the sensations in simple terms: “My heart is beating fast. I feel warm. I feel dizzy.” Try to avoid arguing with the sensations or trying to make them stop.

Let go of the goal of feeling calm right away. The goal is learning, not immediate comfort. Instead of asking, “How do I get rid of this?” practice asking, “Can I make room for this and see what happens?”

It can also help to notice anxious thoughts without treating them as facts. A thought like “I’m going to faint” is a thought, not proof. Over time, you learn to test these predictions against experience.

## Breathing and panic

Many people are told to “take deep breaths” during panic. For some, that helps. For others, repeated deep breathing can worsen symptoms by changing carbon dioxide levels and increasing lightheadedness. In treatment, the focus is usually on steady, gentle breathing rather than large breaths.

A helpful pattern is to breathe slowly and comfortably, allowing the belly to move, without forcing it. If you use breathing skills, use them to support willingness and steadiness, not to escape every sensation immediately.

## Safety and medical concerns

Interoceptive exposure should be planned carefully. Before starting, tell your therapist about any medical conditions, such as heart or lung problems, fainting history, seizures, pregnancy, recent surgery, or other health concerns. Also discuss medications, substance use, and any limits on physical activity.

These exercises are generally designed to create temporary, harmless sensations, but they should be adapted to fit your health needs. If you have symptoms that are new, unusual, severe, or medically concerning, seek medical evaluation rather than assuming they are panic.

## What helps recovery

Recovery is usually not about making anxiety disappear forever. It is about changing your relationship to the sensations and reducing fear, avoidance, and restriction. Progress often comes from repeated practice, patience, and willingness to feel uncomfortable in small, manageable steps.

It is normal to feel nervous about starting exposure. Many people worry that they will not be able to handle it. With guidance and practice, most find that the sensations become less frightening than expected. Confidence grows from doing, not from waiting to feel ready.

Try to measure success by what you practice, not just by whether anxiety showed up. If you stayed with a feared sensation a little longer, reduced a safety behavior, or entered a situation you usually avoid, that is meaningful progress.

## A hopeful outlook

Panic attacks can feel overwhelming, but they are treatable. Interoceptive exposure is a practical, evidence-based approach that helps many people reduce panic and reclaim parts of life that fear has narrowed. By learning to face the body sensations you fear, you can teach your brain that discomfort is not danger and that you are more capable than panic would have you believe.
